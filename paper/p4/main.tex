\begin{abstract}
Multiple local learning rules acting on the same synapses propose incompatible updates; systems
built on a single rule never face this. We ask when reconciliation between such rules can be
\emph{measured}, and answer with a benchmark, a protocol, and one negative result. The
Rules-Collide benchmark places a Hebbian rule and e-prop on the one weight they already share and
scores representation separability: a fixed 50/50 mixture reaches a Fisher ratio of 0.651 against
0.472 and 0.331 for the rules alone (five seeds; paired margin +0.179). Arbitration itself ---
choosing that weight rather than fixing it --- comes back negative, and measurably so: no adaptive
scheme we could build beats the fixed mixture, the static optimum turns out to be the default
value, and the only affordable objective was shown to lack discriminating power before it was
used. Four triviality analyses --- degenerate measurements that could otherwise have been
published as results --- are reported together with the guards that now catch them. The claim is
narrow and, we think, useful: arbitration is not a free improvement on mixing, and whether it can
be measured at all is a question about objectives, not about ideas.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The problem this paper measures}

Three observations motivate the question, and none of them is new; what is new is that the
question is made measurable.

\textbf{Biology.} A single neuron does not use one plasticity rule. In vivo imaging during
learning finds distinct rules operating in different dendritic compartments of the same cell:
apical compartments follow local co-activity in a Hebbian-like form, basal compartments follow
coincidence with postsynaptic spiking in an STDP-like form, and blocking postsynaptic spiking
attenuates the basal plasticity without touching the apical --- a causal dissociation, not a
correlation \citep{wright2025distinct}. Heterogeneous rules coexisting on one neuron is a
biological fact; treating one rule as universal is therefore a modelling assumption, justified
only when a system has one learning signal to serve.

\textbf{Engineering.} Any multi-layer system that carries more than one learning signal --- an
unsupervised structural rule, a supervised error signal, a reward-modulated critic --- eventually
routes them onto shared weights. The rules then propose updates for the same tensor at the same
time, and those updates are not on speaking terms: they are computed from different quantities,
with different signs, on different timescales.

\textbf{Epistemology.} Each rule sees only part of the problem. A Hebbian rule sees input
statistics but not the labels; e-prop sees the error but only where labels exist. Whether
combining them beats either is an empirical question, and empirical questions deserve a benchmark
rather than an intuition.

\subsection{Why arbitration is not hyperparameter tuning}

A hyperparameter is chosen offline from a grid, on data reserved for the purpose, against the same
metric that later judges the model. An arbiter is chosen online, from quantities available during
learning, under constraints that keep it from degenerating. The first is a search; the second is a
control law.

The distinction matters because the first is cheap and the second is not, and because a negative
answer to one is not a negative answer to the other. This paper ends up reporting both: a static
sweep that behaves exactly like hyperparameter search (Section~\ref{sec:pending}), and an online
scheme that had to earn its place against the value the sweep found.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A formalisation} of arbitration as continuous mixing under a two-sided retention
  constraint, with the degenerate endpoints characterised and excluded by protocol rather than by
  intent.
  \item \textbf{The Rules-Collide benchmark}: a task in which two heterogeneous local rules share
  the one weight they already have in common, and in which their fixed mixture separates
  representations far better than either rule alone (0.651 against 0.472 and 0.331).
  \item \textbf{A negative result about arbitration itself}, obtained under pre-registration and
  reported with its price tag: no adaptive scheme we could build beats the fixed mixture, and the
  static optimum turns out to be the default value.
  \item \textbf{Four triviality analyses} --- degeneracies that could have been published as
  results --- together with the guards that now catch each of them.
  \item \textbf{Open assets}: rules, benchmark, pre-registration records with timestamps, and the
  arbitration code.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Switching synaptic plasticity rules}

Turcu et al.\ let synapses \emph{switch} between plasticity rules according to synaptic state,
routing learning signals through a recurrent circuit \citep{turcu2025switching}. This is the
closest neighbour to our question and the clearest contrast in mechanism: their arbitration object
is a discrete selection per synapse, ours is a single continuous weight for the whole tensor.
Two differences bound the comparison, and we state them rather than paper over them. First, a
switching rule can apply one rule's update in full, whereas a convex mixture necessarily attenuates
each contribution (Section~\ref{sec:pending}). Second, their strongest rule involves
backpropagation, while our system is local end to end, including the outer loop that sets the
mixing weight. A matched empirical comparison is future work; it would need budget-matched arms
and a pre-registered criterion, not a demonstration.

\subsection{Meta-learned plasticity}

Confavreux et al.\ meta-learn plasticity rules for recurrent spiking networks with evolution
strategies and report a failure mode that shaped our design: the meta-learning degrades as the
rule parameterisation grows more complex \citep{confavreux2025}. We therefore pinned the arbiter to
a single scalar. Our reported failure is of a different kind --- the parameterisation was adequate,
and what could not be built was an affordable objective that identifies the right value
(Section~\ref{sec:pending}) --- which is worth separating, because the two constraints argue for
different remedies.

Miconi et al.\ meta-learn plasticity through backpropagation \citep{miconi2018differentiable},
which is the mechanism we deliberately avoid: an outer loop that differentiates through the inner
learning rule would puncture the locality claim this project exists to test.

\subsection{Neuromodulation and gating}

Metaplasticity --- a plasticity rule that itself changes with history --- is one way a network can
carry memory in its rules rather than its weights, and it has been analysed as such in multilevel
recurrent networks \citep{zanardi2024metaplasticity}. The distinction from this paper is where the
modulation acts: neuromodulatory accounts gate \emph{one} rule's update, whereas arbitration
combines the outputs of \emph{different} rules. A gated rule can be expressed as a mixture with a
state-dependent weight, which is why the contrast is informative: our negative result constrains
the mixture form, not the gating literature.

\subsection{Multi-objective interference at the synapse}

Continual-learning methods in the regularisation family address interference \emph{between tasks}:
one objective's gradient is constrained so that another's performance survives. The interference
here is different in kind --- it is between two rules proposing updates for the same synapse at the
same instant, before any task boundary exists. Regularisation has no purchase on it, because there
is no previous objective to protect; what is needed is a decision about which update to apply, and
that decision is the arbitration object.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Arbitration formalism}

Two rules $A$ and $B$ propose updates $u_A, u_B \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{pre}} \times n_{\text{post}}}$
for the same weight tensor. The arbiter mixes them with a single scalar,
\begin{equation}
u(\alpha) = (1 - \alpha)\, u_A + \alpha\, u_B, \qquad \alpha \in [0, 1],
\end{equation}
so $\alpha = 0$ is rule $A$ alone and $\alpha = 1$ rule $B$ alone. This is the object the paper
calls arbitration: not a switch, not a schedule, but a weight on a convex combination.

\textbf{Retention is a two-sided constraint.} Both endpoints are degenerate --- one rule is
deleted --- so the protocol constrains $\alpha \in [0.2, 0.8]$: each rule keeps at least 20\% of
the mixture. We report \emph{retention} (the weight actually retained by the suppressed side)
jointly with every conflict-reduction number, because a reduction achieved by deleting a rule is
not arbitration.

\textbf{Conflict is measured per row, not per synapse.} For one synapse the ``direction'' of an
update is a scalar, so the cosine is $\pm 1$ and an angle threshold of $90^\circ$ degenerates to
a sign test (Section~\ref{sec:triviality}, case two). We therefore compute the cosine between the
blended update and each rule's update over the outgoing weights of a presynaptic neuron --- the
smallest group that carries a direction --- and count a conflict event when the cosine is
negative.

\subsection{Meta-learning the arbitration signal}\label{sec:methods-meta}

The arbiter is tuned by a one-parameter evolution strategy, never by backpropagation: the outer
loop perturbs $\alpha$, scores the perturbation, and moves. This keeps the whole system inside the
locality claim (the plan's boundary declaration for the perceptual layer is the same rule), and it
is deliberately minimal --- Confavreux et al.\ report meta-learning failing as rule
parameterisations grow complex \citep{confavreux2025}, so one scalar is the conservative choice.

\textbf{Conditional pre-registration.} If the scalar arbiter shows no gain on this benchmark, a
per-row ($n_{\text{pre}}$-dimensional) extension is not pursued: the parameter space grows by a
factor of $n_{\text{pre}}$ while the premise has already been refuted. The condition is recorded
before the arms ran.

\subsection{The Rules-Collide benchmark}

Rows of sequential MNIST are presented one at a time under a fixed random permutation, so class
information lives in the temporal structure rather than in pixel adjacency. Only 1\% of the
training labels are visible to the supervised rule; the unsupervised rule sees all of the data.
Both rules act on the same $28 \to 256$ input weight --- the synapse the two lines already share ---
and nothing else is mixed.

The two rules are an information-bottleneck Hebbian rule (unsupervised, structure-seeking) and
e-prop (supervised, error-driven). Three arms are compared: $H$ (Hebbian only), $E$ (e-prop only)
and $MIX$ (the blend). The primary metric is the \emph{Fisher ratio} of the representation the
learned weight produces, computed on a held-out probe set: it measures how separable the classes
have become, not how well a particular readout happens to fit, and it does not saturate the way a
linear probe does on random projections.

\textbf{Run gate.} Every run reports $\|\Delta W\| / \|W_0\|$. A run below $0.05$ is void and
excluded from every statistic --- a rule that did not move the weights cannot be credited or blamed
for the outcome, and the exclusion is counted and reported, not silently applied.

\textbf{Criterion.} The synergy margin is $\mathrm{Fisher}(MIX) - \max(\mathrm{Fisher}(H),
\mathrm{Fisher}(E))$, paired by seed, and the pre-registered threshold is a median of
$\delta = 0.05$ together with a separation requirement (the mixture's lower quartile above the
best single arm's upper quartile). Three controls accompany it: the initial weight (the zero
point), a frozen $\alpha = 0.5$ arm, and a shuffled-label arm.

\subsection{Protocol inheritance}

The measurement protocol is inherited wholesale from the companion paper and is not re-derived
here: fixed compute budgets per arm, full configuration dumps with every result, bitwise
reproducibility of a re-run from a stored seed, a frozen random stream for evaluation, selection
on a validation split rather than on the evaluation set, and joint reporting of any metric that can
be improved by a degenerate solution. Each of these exists because its absence produced a wrong
number at least once.

\subsection{Triviality analyses: four cases}\label{sec:triviality}

Four times, a first version of a measurement could be satisfied without the phenomenon it was
meant to detect. They share a shape --- a design, a reason the design is trivial, a guard that
catches it, and a lesson --- and they are reported together because the guards, not the designs,
are the reusable part. Statement and lesson appear below; derivations and parameters are in
Appendix~\ref{app:triviality}.

\textbf{Case one: arbitration that wins by switching a rule off.} If the objective is ``reduce
conflict between the blend and rule $A$'', then $\alpha = 0$ trivially achieves zero conflict. The
guard is the two-sided retention constraint plus joint reporting of retention and reduction.

\textbf{Case two: a per-synapse cosine that cannot have an angle.} On a single synapse the update
is a scalar, the cosine is $\pm 1$, and the $90^\circ$ threshold collapses to a sign test. The
guard is to measure over a row.

\textbf{Case three: an evolutionary search whose objective has no gradient.} An integer
conflict-event count saturates --- at $\alpha = 0.5$ it is already zero, so every perturbation
scores the same and the search never moves. The guard is a discrimination check that every
candidate objective must pass before it may be used.

\textbf{Case four: a proxy that measures a different construct.} Mean agreement with both rules
peaks at $\alpha = 0.9$ and assigns the same value to $\alpha = 0$ and $\alpha = 1$, arms that
differ by $0.14$ in Fisher. Alignment is not separability. The guard is the same discrimination
check, applied to proxies.

\section{Experimental Setup}

Every run reported in this paper was executed on the CPU of a 16-core desktop machine; the
project's GPU was not used, so the numbers are reproducible without one. A single arm-seed takes
about thirteen minutes at the settings below, which is what makes a five-seed grid and a five-point
static sweep affordable in the first place.

The shared backbone is a 256-unit representation fed by a $28 \to 256$ input weight, trained for
40 epochs with batch size 64 on sequential MNIST under a fixed random permutation, with 1\% of the
training labels visible to the supervised rule and five seeds per arm. Inputs are binarised rows;
the representation is the time-averaged rectified projection described in
Section~\ref{sec:synergy}. Fisher ratios are computed on a held-out probe set that is never used
for any selection decision.

Software: Python 3.12 with NumPy and a CPU build of PyTorch; the exact flag sets, seeds and
provenance blocks for every reported number are reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:config}.
\section{Results}
\subsection{Synergy: does the mixture beat both parents?}\label{sec:synergy}

The Rules-Collide benchmark pits three arms against each other on a fixed-permutation
sequential MNIST with 1\% of the training labels: Hebbian alone (H), e-prop alone (E), and a
50/50 mixture of the two (MIX). All three share the same $28 \to 256$ input weight, receive the
same data and the same batches, and are scored by the Fisher ratio of the representation they
produce, evaluated on a held-out probe set. Table~\ref{tab:synergy} reports five seeds per arm.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Representation separability (Fisher ratio) on the Rules-Collide benchmark,
40 epochs, 1\% labels, five seeds. Intervals are the interquartile range.
The paired synergy margin subtracts, per seed, the stronger of the two single-rule arms.}
\label{tab:synergy}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Arm & Fisher (median) & IQR & Seeds used \\
\midrule
H alone (pure Hebbian)   & 0.472 & [0.471, 0.472] & 5/5 \\
E alone (pure e-prop)    & 0.331 & [0.327, 0.337] & 4/5 (one seed failed the run gate) \\
MIX (fixed $\alpha = 0.5$) & \textbf{0.651} & [0.636, 0.652] & 5/5 \\
\midrule
Synergy margin (MIX $-$ best single) & \textbf{+0.179} & [+0.175, +0.182] & 4 paired seeds \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The mixture beats both parents. The pre-registered criterion was a paired median margin of at
least $\delta = 0.05$ together with a separation requirement (the mixture's lower quartile above
the best single arm's upper quartile); both hold, with the margin more than three times the
threshold. One e-prop seed is excluded: its weight moved less than the run gate allows
($\|\Delta W\|/\|W_0\| = 0.0474 < 0.05$), so it is void rather than slow, and the paired margin
is computed on four seeds, which is stated wherever the number appears.

A static sweep of the mixing weight locates the optimum this benchmark allows. Over
$\alpha \in \{0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7\}$ with five seeds each, the Fisher ratio is 0.364
[0.361, 0.450] at 0.3, 0.577 [0.563, 0.627] at 0.4, 0.651 [0.636, 0.652] at 0.5, 0.643
[0.639, 0.643] at 0.6 and 0.621 [0.604, 0.622] at 0.7. The optimum is interior and sits at
equal weights; the adjacent $\alpha = 0.6$ is statistically indistinguishable (paired median
$-0.008$), while 0.4 and 0.7 are clearly worse. Two consequences follow. First, the 50/50
default the benchmark shipped with happens to be at the optimum. Second, an adaptive scheme
has essentially no headroom left to beat it on this task, and that is the question
Section~
ef{sec:pending} asks.

What this result does \emph{not} establish is that \emph{arbitration} is what produced it. The
mixture here is a fixed 50/50 blend; the arm that would adapt the mixing weight is treated
separately in Section~\ref{sec:pending}. Table~\ref{tab:synergy} is therefore a two-level
ordering --- mixture over singles --- and the third level is deliberately left open.
\subsection{Arbitration without degeneracy}\label{sec:pending}

The adaptive arm was run under a pre-registration written before any of its numbers existed,
and the answer is negative. We report it in the order the constraints were discovered.

\textbf{First, no objective could be priced.} The natural objective --- an evolution strategy
climbing on the count of conflict events --- turned out to have no gradient: at $\alpha = 0.5$
the blend already conflicts with the other rule zero times, every perturbation scores the same,
and $\alpha$ never moves; the arm was bit-identical to a frozen $0.5$ in all five seeds. Two
replacements were then checked against a pre-registered discrimination rule and failed it: an
end-to-end Fisher objective has no discriminating power on probes short enough to afford (its
range across $\alpha$ is $0.026$--$0.039$, not growing with probe length), and a smooth proxy
for ``agreement with both rules'' peaks at $\alpha = 0.9$ while assigning the same value to
$\alpha = 0$ and $\alpha = 1$ --- arms that differ by $0.14$ in Fisher. Adaptive arbitration is
therefore \emph{not measurable by any objective we could afford}; the only known-valid objective,
a full-course probe, is priced at about seven hours per fit and is left as future work.

\textbf{Second, the static optimum is itself the default.} A sweep of the mixing weight over
$\alpha \in \{0.3, \dots, 0.7\}$ (Section~\ref{sec:synergy}) locates the optimum at equal
weights. A frozen $0.5$ is therefore not a straw man but the best static setting this benchmark
admits.

\textbf{Third, slow adaptation moves, and moves the wrong way.} With no outer optimizer and no
probe, we let $\alpha(t)$ drift at routine validation checkpoints (step $0.05$, interval
$[0.2, 0.8]$, every five epochs, validation carved from the training split so that the choice
never touches the evaluation set). The trajectory is monotone and identical in shape across all
five seeds: $0.50 \to 0.55 \to \dots \to 0.80$, parking at the upper bound. Final Fisher is
$0.642$ against $0.632$ for a matched fixed-$0.5$ control trained on the same split --- a paired
median of $+0.003$, below the pre-registered $\delta = 0.05$ --- and $0.651$ for the full-data
frozen $0.5$, i.e.\ \emph{worse} by $0.009$. The drift tracks a \emph{transient} validation
signal rather than the final optimum, and it ends by parking on the constraint boundary --- the
same family as the two degeneracies of Section~\ref{sec:triviality}: a quantity that is
optimised, but not the quantity that matters.

\textbf{What the two arms established, stated separately.} They answer different questions and
should not be read as one result. The static sweep answers \emph{how much room adaptation has}:
none, because the optimum is the value a system would pick without measuring. The slow-adaptive arm
answers \emph{whether an adaptive scheme can take the room it has}: under the pre-registered
criterion, adaptive $\approx$ static --- a paired median of $+0.003$ against a matched
$50/50$ control, an order of magnitude below $\delta = 0.05$ --- and what adaptation did instead
was drift away from the optimum and park on the constraint boundary.

\textbf{The line is sealed.} The pre-registration allowed these two arms and no third, and its
own conditional clause is therefore triggered: because the scalar arbiter showed no gain, the
per-row extension is \emph{not pursued} (Section~\ref{sec:methods-meta}). The conclusion is not
``untested'' but \emph{measured and negative}: on this benchmark no adaptive arbitration scheme we
could build beats a fixed 50/50 mixture, and the best static mixture is itself 50/50. What remains
open is narrower and better posed: whether an objective exists that is affordable \emph{and}
discriminating, which we have shown is the binding constraint rather than the arbitration idea
itself.
\subsection{Continuous mixing vs.\ discrete switching}

The closest neighbour switches plasticity rules \emph{discretely}, synapse by synapse
\citep{turcu2025switching}; we mix them \emph{continuously} with one scalar. We did not run a
matched empirical comparison, and we do not claim one: the conditions differ (their strongest
rule includes backpropagation, ours is local end to end including the outer loop), so a side-by-side
number would compare two different claims at once. What we can state analytically is where the two
formulations differ, and why we chose ours.

\textbf{State space.} Discrete switching gives each synapse a categorical state; for $n$ rules the
per-synapse state space is $n^{|W|}$, and the arbitration object is a selector. Continuous mixing
gives each synapse the \emph{same} scalar $\alpha$, so the arbitration object is one number for the
entire network. The second is a much smaller hypothesis class --- a feature for a paper whose
subject is measurement, a limitation for a paper whose subject is capacity.

\textbf{Attenuation.} Switching applies one rule's update in full; mixing applies a convex
combination, so each rule's contribution is scaled by its weight. In our benchmark this shows up
directly: the mixture's weight displacement per run ($\|\Delta W\|/\|W_0\| \approx 0.41$) sits
between pure Hebbian's ($\approx 0.71$) and pure e-prop's ($\approx 0.05$), i.e.\ mixing costs
magnitude relative to the dominant rule. Whether switching's fuller updates are worth the larger
state space is an empirical question this paper does not answer.

\textbf{Searchability.} A continuous parameter has an interior optimum that a one-dimensional
search can locate --- which is exactly what Section~\ref{sec:pending} did, and exactly how it
found that the optimum is the default. A discrete selector has no such notion of
``how much'', and its search space does not admit the same statement.

A matched comparison is left as future work, with the caveat stated above: it would require
budget-matching the two arms and a pre-registered criterion, not a demonstration.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{When does arbitration pay?}

The benchmark answers a narrower question than the one it was built to ask, and the narrowing is
the result. \emph{Mixing} pays whenever the rules are complementary: pure Hebbian reaches a Fisher
ratio of 0.472 and pure e-prop 0.331, while their fixed blend reaches 0.651 --- a margin of 0.179
that no single-rule arm approaches. \emph{Arbitration} --- choosing the weight rather than fixing
it --- did not pay here, for a reason that is now specific rather than mysterious: the optimum of
the static mixture is the default value, so the quantity an arbiter would estimate has no room to
move.

This suggests a criterion that can be applied before building an arbiter, in the same syntax as
P2's criterion for when local rules suffice: \textbf{arbitration is worth measuring only if the
static optimum is unknown \emph{and} away from the value one would pick without measuring.} Where
the optimum sits at the default, adaptivity buys nothing and the benchmark cannot distinguish a
good arbiter from a lucky constant.

\subsection{The 1-scalar finding}

Confavreux et al.\ report meta-learning failing as rule parameterisations grow complex, and our
arbiter was deliberately pinned to one scalar to stay on the safe side of that finding
\citep{confavreux2025}. The failure we hit was of a different kind, and worth separating: it was
not that one scalar was too coarse to express the right arbitration --- the static sweep shows the
optimum really is a single number, and that number is 0.5. It was that \emph{no affordable
objective could identify that number}. An integer conflict count saturates; a short-horizon Fisher
probe cannot see the effect it is meant to search; a smooth agreement proxy ranks the endpoints
identically. The bottleneck was not parameterisation size but objective identifiability, which is a
constraint on the outer loop rather than on the arbitration idea.

\subsection{Limits}

All conclusions are limited to a two-rule pair. Three-way mixing was not tested and is out of
scope by design: with more than two rules the definition of conflict itself needs re-design (pairwise
per-row cosines are no longer unique), the number of pairs grows quadratically, and a scheduling
dimension appears. The project roadmap places three-way mixing and scheduling in a later phase.

The benchmark is one task: sequential MNIST under a fixed permutation, 1\% labels, 256 neurons,
five seeds. We did not test other label fractions, other permutations, or larger networks, and the
numbers should not be extrapolated to them. The slow-adaptive arm additionally reserves 10\% of the
training split for validation; its control arm uses the same split, so the comparison is internal,
but it is not directly comparable to the full-data static runs.

\textbf{Compartments, not mixtures.} The in vivo result that motivates this paper
\citep{wright2025distinct} can also be read against it: the brain does not appear to blend
heterogeneous rules on shared synapses --- it separates them across dendritic compartments. We
take that seriously and treat it as a different point in the design space rather than a
counter-example. Compartmentalisation buys separation at the cost of structural change: rules must
be physically partitioned, and a synapse cannot benefit from a rule its compartment does not
express. Mixing on shared synapses is the complementary choice --- no new structure, but the rules
must now settle their disagreements numerically. This benchmark measures the second choice; the
first is future work, and the comparison between them is the interesting experiment neither this
paper nor that one runs.

Finally, nothing here was run on neuromorphic hardware; the locality claim concerns the learning
rule, not the substrate.

\section{Conclusion}

Rules conflict on shared synapses, and the conflict is measurable: a fixed mixture of two
heterogeneous local rules separates representations far better than either rule alone
(0.651 against 0.472 and 0.331), in a network with no backpropagation anywhere, under a
protocol that pre-registers its criteria and voids runs whose weights did not move.

The title asks when arbitration can be measured, and the answer has three layers.
\textbf{Mixing is measurable and measured}: the mixture beats both parents by a margin that no
seed-level noise explains. \textbf{Adaptation is measurable in principle but not at a price we
could pay}: the only objective known to rank candidates the way the acceptance metric does requires
a probe long enough to be a full training run, priced at about seven hours per fit; the adaptive
scheme that \emph{was} affordable produced no gain over a fixed mix.
\textbf{The protocol is itself the instrument}: four degeneracies were caught by pre-registered
guards before any of them could reach a reader as a result.

Arbitration itself, under the two-sided retention constraint and a one-scalar parameterisation,
came back negative on this benchmark --- measurably negative, not merely untested: the static
optimum is the default value, and the one adaptive scheme that could run without an unaffordable
objective drifted away from that optimum and parked on the constraint boundary. We report the
negative result with the same care as the positive one, because the reusable part is the protocol
that produced it: four degeneracies caught by their own guards, and a criterion that can be
checked before an arbiter is built.

The next step is the cognitive-core integration on the project roadmap, and --- for this line
specifically --- an objective that is both affordable and discriminating.

\section{Triviality analysis details}\label{app:triviality}

\textbf{Case one.} With conflict measured between the blend $(1-\alpha)u_A + \alpha u_B$ and
$u_A$, the count is zero at $\alpha = 0$ by construction, so the phase-two criterion
(``$\ge 50\%$ reduction'') is satisfied at the degenerate endpoint. Hence the two-sided constraint
and joint reporting.

\textbf{Case two.} For a single synapse $u \in \mathbb{R}$,
$\cos(u_A, u_B) = \mathrm{sign}(u_A)\mathrm{sign}(u_B) \in \{-1, +1\}$ whenever both are non-zero.
The threshold set $\{\cos < 0\}$ is therefore invariant to magnitude and rotation: there is no
interior to threshold.

\textbf{Case three.} The integer objective is a step function of $\alpha$ with a plateau that
contains the starting value; a hill climb that accepts only strict improvements cannot leave a
plateau. Measured: five seeds, five identical trajectories, final $\alpha$ exactly $0.5$.
Calibration of the alternative objective, on a trained snapshot, Fisher across
$\alpha \in \{0.2, 0.35, 0.5, 0.65, 0.8\}$: range 0.026--0.039 at probe lengths 8, 50, 200 and 800
steps --- flat at every length, hence no usable gradient.

\textbf{Case four.} The mean-cosine proxy over a grid of $\alpha$ (twenty steps of 200 batches,
seed 0): 0.501 at $\alpha=0$, rising monotonically to 0.625 at $\alpha=0.9$, and 0.501 again at
$\alpha=1$. Fisher over the same range peaks at $\alpha = 0.5$ with 0.651 against 0.472 and 0.331
at the endpoints.
