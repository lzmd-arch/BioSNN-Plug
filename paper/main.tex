\begin{abstract}
Local learning rules for spiking neural networks are usually compared across
papers rather than within one, and rarely at matched budgets. We benchmark three
purely local rule lines --- e-prop on a recurrent spiking network, kernelized
information-bottleneck Hebbian feature learning, and reward-modulated STDP with
a TD-LTP critic --- under one measurement protocol, on one consumer GPU. Four
findings, two of them uncomfortable. The neuron model is not a detail: with
everything else fixed, a non-adaptive LIF neuron beats the adaptive ALIF neuron
at \emph{every} learning rate we swept (0.93--1.23 pp, five seeds each). e-prop
with LIF reaches 0.8889 on sequential MNIST, but only after an eight-fold budget
extension. A matched-budget backpropagation-through-time control, each side tuned
at its own best recipe, reaches \textbf{0.9630} --- 7.41 points ahead, the
largest effect measured anywhere in this paper. And the same local pipeline does
not learn SHD at all (5.34\% against a 5\% chance level). We contribute the
protocol, the bit-exact reproductions behind every number, and an analysis of the
degenerate solutions a benchmark of this kind invites.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Motivation}
Training a recurrent spiking network by backpropagation through time (BPTT)
costs two things that a biological or an on-chip learner is not supposed to
spend. The first is memory: the backward pass needs the forward activation
history of every time step, which grows with sequence length. The second is a
gradient that has to be defined at all --- spikes are threshold events, so the
backward pass runs through a surrogate whose choice is itself a
hyperparameter. Local learning rules avoid both by construction: a synapse
computes its own update from quantities it can see, presynaptic and postsynaptic
activity together with a signal broadcast to the whole network
\citep{bellec2020eprop}. That is the promise. What the promise does not come
with is a measurement convention, and this paper is about the second thing.

\subsection{The measurement gap}
The local-rule literature reports accuracies, and the conditions under which
they were obtained are not a required part of the report --- optimizer, seed
count, budget, and what evaluation does with encoding noise. The consequence is
visible in the numbers themselves. A widely cited comparison table carries rows
it did not run, footnoted as taken from a third party, so a reader comparing
against it is comparing protocols rather than rules (Section~\ref{sec:related}).
A rule can be reported at one configuration with its headline accuracy and no
independent reproduction. And a benchmark that controls architecture and dataset
can still leave the compute budget and seed count of the methods it compares
undisclosed. None of this is misconduct; it is what happens when measurement
conventions are left implicit.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Honest benchmark}: three local-rule lines --- Hebbian feature
    learning, e-prop, and reward-modulated STDP --- under one protocol, with
    budgets, seeds and evaluation procedure public. Headline results:
    W1 Hebbian features reach \textbf{0.9774} over five seeds (range 0.14 pp);
    W2 e-prop with LIF converges at \textbf{0.8889} on sequential MNIST after
    250 epochs; W3 R-STDP with a TD-LTP critic reaches a median of
    \textbf{237.8} steps on CartPole over twenty held-out seeds.
  \item \textbf{Interaction finding}: neuron type $\times$ learning rate swept as
    a controlled grid; the adaptation term is a net drag at every learning rate
    tested (same-budget LIF leads ALIF by 0.93--1.23 pp over lr $10^{-3}$ to
    $10^{-2}$; five seeds each).
  \item \textbf{Convergence evidence}: e-prop with LIF neurons converges at
    \textbf{0.8889} on sequential MNIST (250 epochs), with the plateau criterion
    pre-registered before the run and met. What that number is worth against a
    backpropagation control is Section~\ref{sec:results}'s to say, and it is the
    paper's largest measured effect; the gap is deliberately not restated here,
    because a number should have exactly one home.
  \item \textbf{Methodology}: bit-exact reproduction (two independent runs of the
    same configuration agree at every epoch, not just in the mean),
    retention-joint reporting, degenerate-solution analysis, and a frozen-noise
    evaluation protocol.
  \item \textbf{A negative result, in the open}: the same e-prop configuration
    does not learn SHD (5.34\% against a 5\% chance level), reported with its
    attribution and its gap to the literature.
  \item \textbf{Open assets}: all code, configurations and scripts are public
    (the skeleton library on PyPI); the evidence ledger behind every number is
    versioned with the paper's source.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Positioning: what this paper is not}
Three non-claims. First, we make no claim about which learning rule is best in
general: we measure three specific rule lines at one scale on one device, and
the comparison is only valid inside that setting. Second, we claim nothing about
emergence, cognition or intelligence --- a network that classifies a permuted
frame sequence is not evidence about either. Third, we do not hide the negative
results: the SHD arm of this work failed, and it is reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}
with the same detail as the parts that worked. What we do claim is narrower and
checkable: under a stated protocol, these rules produce these numbers, and the
numbers move when the protocol changes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Local learning rules for spiking networks}
A family of learning rules for spiking neural networks (SNNs) restricts synaptic
updates to quantities available at the synapse --- presynaptic and postsynaptic
activity together with a broadcast modulation signal --- thereby avoiding
backpropagation through time (BPTT) and its requirement to store complete
activation histories. We refer to these as \emph{local learning rules} and
review the five most directly relevant lines of work.

\citet{bellec2020eprop} introduced e-prop, a three-factor rule in which each
synapse maintains a decaying eligibility trace computed from local spike
events, while a learning signal broadcast from the readout layer supplies the
third factor. The rule requires O(1) memory in sequence length and was shown to
approach BPTT-level performance on speech and sequence tasks with adaptive
LIF (ALIF) neurons. Published e-prop results, however, are typically reported
at a single hyperparameter setting with a small number of seeds, leaving the
rule's sensitivity to optimization choices largely uncharacterized. In this
work we treat such sensitivity as a first-class measurement target rather
than a nuisance variable.

\citet{pes2025tp} move the trace from the synapse to the neuron: traces
propagation (TP) keeps two decaying activity traces per neuron rather than an
eligibility trace per synapse, and derives the learning signal from a layer-wise
contrastive loss, for a space complexity of $O(LH)$ against the $LH^{2}$ the
same paper tabulates for e-prop. Its measurements, however, are taken on fully
connected feedforward architectures with LIF neurons, and part of the SHD
comparison it prints is transcribed from a third party rather than re-run under
its own protocol --- the paper footnotes those rows as such. TP therefore does
not establish that the per-synapse traces which make e-prop's storage quadratic
can be avoided in a recurrent core, which is the setting we measure; on the
paper's own N-MNIST table it is e-prop, not TP, that takes the higher number
(97.90 against 97.33). We accordingly treat TP as a parallel rule rather than a
drop-in replacement, and do not use it as the storage argument for our
recurrent model. The same memory question has since been answered at the system
level rather than at the level of the rule: BrainTrace \citep{wang2026braintrace}
compiles a family of online rules --- D-RTRL and pp-prop among them --- onto
user-specified spiking models, and reports linear memory in sequence length
together with an online fit of a whole-brain-scale \textit{Drosophila} network.
That is a toolchain contribution, measured in memory footprint, throughput and
scale; closer to our setting, it does not report what any single rule does at a
fixed accuracy budget, which is the axis this paper measures.

\citet{espp2024} take a different route: EchoSpike predictive plasticity (ESPP)
uses the whole spike train of the previous sample as a prediction target, and
pulls same-label samples together while pushing different-label ones apart, a
layer-local rule that never invokes automatic differentiation. The rule also
decides for itself which time steps are worth updating --- a threshold on input
activity plus a threshold on the loss --- and the authors report that weight
updates are executed on only 18--27\% of time steps, a fraction that decreases
during training, alongside an SHD accuracy of 84.32\%. Both figures come from a
single configuration and are self-reported; we are not aware of an independent
reproduction. ESPP is also not an e-prop variant: it is a
predictive/contrastive layer-local rule, and the selective updating it performs
is a different mechanism from the event-driven form discussed next, with which
it is easily confused.

\citet{korcsak2025event} keep the rule and change when it is evaluated: a
synapse updates only when it receives a spike event, and the implementation is
embedded in a large-scale simulation framework (NEST). It reproduces the
original rule's performance on pattern generation, evidence accumulation and
N-MNIST, and weak- and strong-scales to two million neurons on sparse networks;
to satisfy strict locality it also removes one violation of that principle in
the original rule --- the eligibility filter as published depends on the time
constant of the output neuron, which a synapse cannot know. Those are
measurements taken at HPC scale, and the regime we can afford is a single
consumer GPU with networks roughly four orders of magnitude smaller, where the
question is not whether event-driven updating scales but whether it changes the
numbers at all. Closer to our setting, this is the variant we would reach for
first if scale became the binding constraint; it is not, however, the object of
the benchmark below, which covers the form we can reproduce bit-for-bit on one
machine.

\citet{millidge2025deep} generalize the trace recursion itself, from a single
recurrent layer to arbitrary depth and arbitrary DAGs, arguing that the cost
stays linear in depth. The note contains no experiments --- its author states
that none were run --- so it can support the methodological claim that a
multi-layer recurrent SNN is trainable by purely local rules in principle, and
cannot support any accuracy figure. Its own stated limitations cut against the
word ``online'': e-prop as specified updates weights at the end of an episode
rather than during it, and keeping one set of traces per parameter group becomes,
in the author's phrase, quickly unmanageable with depth. We cite it for those two
statements and for nothing measured.

\subsection{Meta-learned plasticity and arbitration}
A second line does not ask which local rule to use but how the rules themselves
should change: plasticity becomes a learned or slowly varying quantity.

\citet{confavreux2025} meta-learn co-activity plasticity rules for recurrent
spiking networks with an evolutionary-strategy outer loop, over
parameterizations of increasing complexity. Their result is itself a caution:
beyond a certain complexity the meta-learned strategies begin to fail, and
specifying a loss that constrains the emerging dynamics a priori is hard. We do
not meta-learn --- the rules stay fixed and any arbitration between them is
specified by hand --- so the question we can measure is a different one: what a
fixed mixture buys at a matched optimization budget.

\citet{miconi2018differentiable} sit at the other end of the same axis, training
the plasticity coefficients themselves by backpropagation through a plastic
network. That is meta-learning with a global gradient, and we cite it as a
contrast rather than as support: its results say nothing about what purely local
rules achieve, which is the question this paper is built around.

\citet{zanardi2024metaplasticity} analyse metaplasticity as a memory mechanism
rather than as a training algorithm: on a cyclic feed-forward network with
several levels, where a Hebbian term and a metaplastic reinforcement term act on
grouped edges, the dynamics separate into three regimes, and in two of them a
stored path can still be retrieved after the weights have been reset to zero ---
retention that survives at the slower level after the fast one has been erased.
That is the mechanism behind the retention constraint we impose when rules are
mixed; it supplies no accuracy number, and we borrow none.

\citet{turcu2025switching} are the closest neighbour. Their synapses change
their governing plasticity rule independently during training, switching between
a weak Hebbian-like pre-post rule for weak synapses and a strong
credit-assignment rule for strong ones, which the abstract labels backpropagation
(BP); the switching network
is reported to learn with fewer examples than a backpropagation-only baseline,
and to develop a more antisymmetric recurrent weight matrix. Two differences
separate it from our setting. They switch discretely between rules where we mix
two rule updates continuously, and their strong rule is labelled backpropagation,
so the mixture is not itself purely local. Closer to our setting, the question that
remains is the one we pose: what must arbitration satisfy so that mixing two
updates does not degrade what either rule has already retained.

\subsection{SNN--LLM hybrid architectures}
A third line couples a spiking network to a large language model and divides the
work between them. In EMBER \citep{savage2026ember} the SNN is the substrate and
the LLM the swappable engine: the spiking network decides when to act and which
associations surface, the language model decides what the action or the sentence
is. Its learning rule is depression-dominated STDP ($A_- = 1.05\,A_+$,
$\tau_+ = 20$~ms, $\tau_- = 30$~ms) with an eligibility trace gated by a global
dopamine signal --- no backpropagation, and no e-prop. The evidence, however, is a
single run without error bars: the report carries N=1, code was promised at
publication and has not appeared, and the author flags LLM confabulation as a
scoring risk in the same document. Two further projects take the complementary
split, letting the spiking network decide what enters the context rather than when
to act. Javis \citep{javis2026} stores knowledge as cell assemblies and recovers
them by pattern completion from a partial cue, using STDP-family rules only.
MEMBRAIN \citep{membrain2026} replaces the representation stage with FlyHash
sparse encoding over Nengo populations and cleans up with attractor dynamics.
Both are self-reported: neither has a paper or a DOI, neither has been reproduced
by a third party, and each README is contradicted by its own repository's
benchmark --- MEMBRAIN's headline completion figure sits alongside 0.35 in its own
store at 0.2 noise, and Javis records that both of its reward arms stayed at
chance. We cite them as precedents for the architecture and for nothing measured.

\subsection{Reproducibility protocols in SNN training}
Machine learning as a field has a protocol-level answer to this problem, and a
recent one: the NeurIPS 2019 reproducibility program added a code submission
policy, a community-wide reproducibility challenge, and the checklist that is now
standard at submission time \citep{pineau2021}. We searched for the equivalent in
local-rule SNN training and did not find one; the sources, query strings, date and
inclusion criteria are recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:search}, and no entry meets
all three criteria for counting as a standard --- with one candidate that could not
be opened at all, recorded there as an open item rather than assumed away. What
the search did return is a set
of near neighbours, and naming them is the honest form of the claim. Benchmarking
frameworks now fix tasks and metrics for comparing SNN learning algorithms,
including e-prop, with stated fairness goals \citep{neuroseqbench2025}, and for
neuromorphic computing more broadly across algorithm and system tracks
\citep{neurobench2023}; spiking transformers have their own evaluation platform
\citep{step2025}. Closest in theme is a benchmark that stratifies SNN learning
methods by locality \citep{benchmarks2024locality}: it controls architecture and
dataset, but the compute budget, seed count and optimizer of the compared methods
are not disclosed --- we differ in that our target is measurement protocol
discipline rather than method comparison. On the simulation side, reproducibility
guidance for spiking network models predates all of this, and it already documents
the phenomenon our protocol is built around: runs differing only in random seed
land in different dynamical states, 87\% against 13\% \citep{pauli2018reproducing}.
What none of them prescribes is what a report of local-rule training must contain
--- optimizer, seed count, budget, and what evaluation does with encoding noise
--- and those are the conditions under which the accuracies surveyed above were
obtained. This is the gap Section~3 is built to fill.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Systems under test}
Three rule lines, each taken from its source paper and implemented independently
of it, are measured.

\textbf{W1: Hebbian feature learning.} A kernelized information-bottleneck
Hebbian layer \citep{pogodin2020kernelized}: features are learned locally,
without backpropagation through the network, with divisive normalization. The
layer is rate-based by construction --- the source rule is defined on continuous
activations --- and we do not convert it to spikes, because doing so would make
the implementation no longer the rule we set out to measure. A linear readout
maps the features to classes, and two readouts are reported: a closed-form ridge
solution and a cross-entropy SGD head. \emph{The reported accuracy is therefore a
joint result of local feature learning and supervised readout training}; it is
not the accuracy of the local rule alone, and no claim in this paper treats it
as such.

\textbf{W2: e-prop on a recurrent spiking network.} The rule of
\citet{bellec2020eprop}: each synapse keeps an eligibility trace built from
local spike events, multiplied by a learning signal broadcast from the readout.
The network is a single recurrent layer of 256 neurons with a linear readout,
trained on sequential MNIST (784 time steps, 10 classes) with a cross-entropy
loss. The neuron model is a switch, not a detail: $\beta = 0$ gives a
non-adaptive LIF neuron and $\beta = 0.07$ an adaptive LIF (ALIF) one, and
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports both. The readout has its own learning rate,
mirroring the source implementation.

\textbf{W3: R-STDP with a TD-LTP critic.} A reward-modulated STDP actor with a
critic trained by TD-LTP \citep{fremaux2013}. The rule's name and form come from
the 2013 paper, not the 2010 one: the earlier paper is where the \emph{bias} of
reward-modulated STDP is analysed, and that analysis is what Section~\ref{sec:results}
reproduces structurally \citep{fremaux2010}. Two honest deviations
from the source: this implementation is \emph{rate-based} rather than spiking
(the spiking form is scheduled, not done --- see Section~\ref{sec:discussion}),
and the critic is a population rather than a single unit, which is what made it
learn at all. The task is CartPole with the environment's own reward, and the
acceptance quantity is the mean number of steps over ten greedy episodes on the
final weights.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
This section is the paper's actual contribution, so it is written out rather
than summarised.

\textbf{(a) Budget discipline.} Every comparison in Section~\ref{sec:results} is
made at a matched budget: same epoch count, same sample count, same seeds. Where
two numbers in this paper were produced at different budgets they are never
subtracted --- the difference between them is not a difference between methods.
A gap reported across budgets is labelled as an observation, not as a result,
and the one place where the literature's budget differs from ours is called out
in the text rather than smoothed over.

\textbf{(b) Configuration dumps.} Every run writes a machine-readable
configuration alongside its results, and the acceptance numbers are queryable:
each figure in this paper traces to a directory of per-run JSON files, and each
JSON records the full configuration that produced it. No number in this paper
exists only inside a table.

\textbf{(c) Seeds and bit-exact reproduction.} Comparisons use five seeds unless
stated otherwise (W2's extended budget uses three, W3's acceptance batch uses
twenty). Reproduction is checked more strictly than the mean: the same
configuration run twice independently reproduces \emph{every} per-epoch value,
not merely the final accuracy, and a learning-rate arm has been re-run three
weeks later on a differently loaded machine with all five per-seed accuracies
identical to four decimal places. Bit-exactness is a property of the pipeline
under a fixed seed, and we state it because a benchmark that cannot reproduce
itself cannot ask the field to.

\textbf{(d) Frozen-noise evaluation.} Training is stochastic. Evaluation is not:
the encoding noise is drawn from a seed fixed at evaluation time, so two runs
that differ only in their training seed are scored on the same inputs.

\textbf{(e) Model selection on validation only.} Hyperparameters are chosen on a
validation split; the test set is read once, at the end, for the configuration
that was selected. Where a tuning batch and an acceptance batch exist, they use
\emph{disjoint seed ranges} and both are reported --- including the gap between
them, which is a selection effect and is disclosed as one rather than averaged
away.

\textbf{(f) Joint reporting.} Any quantity that can be improved by doing nothing
is reported together with the quantity that such a solution would sacrifice. The
degenerate cases we found are enumerated in Section~\ref{sec:triviality}; the
rule they generate is general, and it is why Section~\ref{sec:results} reports
conflict or retention measures in pairs rather than singly.

\subsection{Triviality analysis}
\label{sec:triviality}
Two degenerate solutions were found on synthetic data before they could be
mistaken for results, and they set the reporting rules above.

\emph{Toward-zero mixing.} If an arbitration objective asks only that the mixed
update conflict less with one rule, the optimum is to stop using the other rule
entirely. On synthetic traces with two directly opposed rules, five seeds all
showed a 100\% conflict reduction --- with the mixing coefficient drifting from
0.5 to 0.2--0.3. The reduction was a symptom, not a result: the metric is
trivially satisfied by the solution that does the least. Hence the joint
reporting rule: a reduction is only reported alongside what the other rule still
retains.

\emph{Degenerate angle measurement.} Cosine similarity between two rules'
updates is meaningless per synapse: a single scalar has only a sign, so the
cosine is $\pm 1$ and "angle greater than $90^\circ$" collapses into "opposite
signs". The angle becomes informative only when taken across a group of
synapses --- per row (all outgoing weights of one presynaptic neuron) or per
column --- and the two conventions answer different questions. This paper's
numbers use the per-row convention; mixing the two silently changes what is
being measured.

\subsection{Ethical and boundary statement}
One boundary governs how the results may be read. Where a system contains a
component that was not trained locally --- a supervised readout, a frozen
pre-trained encoder --- the accuracy is a property of the \emph{composition},
and we say so at the point where the number appears (W1's readout is the case in
hand). No system in this paper contains a pre-trained component of any kind:
everything reported here was trained from scratch on the task, on one machine.

The second boundary is scale. All measurements here are at the scale of a single
consumer GPU and networks of a few hundred neurons. Nothing in this paper
demonstrates that the same rules behave the same way at the scale where their
claims would matter most, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} states what would be
needed to find out.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
All experiments ran on one machine: an \textbf{NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5060 with
8{,}151 MiB} of device memory (compute capability sm\_120), on Windows, with
Python $\geq$ 3.11, \texttt{torch}, \texttt{torchvision} and \texttt{torchaudio}
from the CUDA 13.0 wheel index, \texttt{spikingjelly} 2.0.0rc1 and
\texttt{gymnasium} for the research code. The skeleton library used for the
plugin-facing parts is independent of all of this and depends only on
\texttt{numpy} --- the GPU stack is a research dependency, not an architectural one.

\textbf{Energy.} We do not report energy. There is no power monitor on this
machine, and inferring energy from nominal TDP would produce a number that looks
measured and is not. What is reported instead is wall-clock time and peak device
memory, both recorded per run: the largest peak we observed is 3{,}220.6 MiB
(the SHD arm, whose time dimension is 100 steps), against the 8 GiB budget.
Resource behaviour as a function of network size is measured over six layer sizes
in Section~\ref{sec:results}; what remains unmeasured is anything beyond 2{,}048
neurons, which is where the device itself gives out. See Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{W1: Hebbian feature learning}
The locally learned features classify MNIST well, and the two readouts bracket
how much of that is the readout's doing:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrp{0.44\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
Readout & Accuracy & What it costs \\
\midrule
Ridge, closed form (default) & 0.9779 & one solve over accumulated statistics \\
Cross-entropy + SGD & 0.9801 & backpropagation, learning rate, momentum, 100 epochs \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{W1 MNIST accuracy. Both numbers are from the acceptance run (seed 0);
over five seeds the ridge configuration averages 0.9774 with a range of 0.14 pp.
Both are the joint result of locally learned features and a supervised readout;
the 0.22 pp difference is what the supervised head buys.}
\label{tab:w1}
\end{table}

Over five seeds the ridge configuration gives a mean of \textbf{0.9774}
(0.9765--0.9779, range 0.14 pp). The comparison that matters for the boundary
statement is not ridge versus SGD but the fact that both are supervised: the
local rule supplies features, not decisions.

An ablation over five arms at seed 0 reproduces the acceptance configuration
exactly (0.9779) and answers the one question the source paper's tables raise,
namely which of its columns come from which mechanism. One arm, divisive
normalization disabled, \emph{diverged}: its local objective bottomed out at
epoch 10 and climbed thereafter, so the number it produced is not a valid
reading and is not reported as one. An arm that fails is a result; an arm that
diverges and is quoted anyway is not.

\subsection{W2: e-prop sequential learning}
\subsubsection{Learning-rate sweep}
Sequential MNIST, 30 epochs, five seeds, identical in every respect except the
learning rate and the neuron model:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Learning rate & ALIF ($\beta=0.07$) & LIF ($\beta=0$) & LIF $-$ ALIF \\
\midrule
$10^{-3}$ & 0.7564 & \textbf{0.7657} & $+0.93$ pp \\
$2\times10^{-3}$ & 0.7764 & \textbf{0.7867} & $+1.03$ pp \\
$5\times10^{-3}$ & 0.7983 & \textbf{0.8088} & $+1.05$ pp \\
$10^{-2}$ & 0.8002 & \textbf{0.8125} & $+1.23$ pp \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{W2 learning-rate sweep, five seeds per cell. The LIF rows were run
after the ALIF rows, at matched seeds, on the same machine. The last column is
the difference of the two accuracies \emph{as printed}, so every row can be
checked by subtracting the row --- computing it from the unrounded means instead
would move one entry by 0.01 pp.}
\label{tab:w2-lr}
\end{table}

Two things are worth separating here. The learning rate matters far more than
the neuron model --- moving from $2\times10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$ alone is
$+2.38$ pp, an order of magnitude more than any neuron-model difference --- and
that is precisely why a single-setting report cannot be compared with another
single-setting report. Independent of that, the interaction is real and goes
the way the e-prop literature's framing does not predict: at every learning rate
the adaptive neuron is the slower one.

The $2\times10^{-3}$ arm reproduces the results obtained three weeks earlier,
on a machine in a different load state, with all five per-seed accuracies
identical to four decimal places. That is a stronger statement than a matching
mean, and it is the evidence behind the protocol's bit-exactness claim.

\subsubsection{Neuron type interaction}
Table~\ref{tab:w2-lr} is the interaction: five seeds in each cell, and the
intervals do not overlap at any learning rate. The adaptation term is not
neutral at this scale, it is a drag --- the same architecture with $\beta=0$ is
better everywhere on the grid. We probed past the grid edge before reporting
this: a pre-registered probe at $2\times10^{-2}$ was run to test whether
$10^{-2}$ was merely where the grid stopped rather than a working point, and
LIF's curve was flat between the two (the probe improved the mean by 0.14 pp,
inside the noise, against the 0.37 pp the previous step bought). The official

5.9--6.1 hours per seed at 250 epochs, against 25--30 minutes per seed at 30

\textbf{The measurement contradicts the expectation we built it on.} Fitting
log-log slopes over the six points (Figure~\ref{fig:w2-scale}) gives
\textbf{0.86 for memory} and 1.15 for time. Memory therefore grows
\emph{sub}-linearly here, not quadratically.

The traces are not absent from that peak, and the honest accounting matters.
e-prop's eligibility traces are stored per synapse, so their footprint scales as
$N^2$. By this project's own accounting --- four per-synapse tensors, cross-checked
against the implementation's stated sizes at the acceptance shape --- that is about
2.0 GB at $n_{\text{rec}} = 2{,}048$ with batch 64 in fp32. Against an observed peak
of 11.7 GB, the traces are therefore roughly a fifth of it; at $n_{\text{rec}} = 64$
they are one part in three hundred. They are present, and their share grows faster
than anything else in the sweep (from 0.3\% to 17.7\% across 32-fold growth in
size), but they are not what fills the card at these sizes. Something sub-linear is,
and at 784 time steps that term is large enough to hide them for now.

So this curve does \emph{not} corroborate the per-synapse trace accounting that
Section~\ref{sec:related} reports from the source paper --- it is consistent with
it, and it shows the trace term climbing towards dominance, while leaving the
observed peak explained by something else entirely. Establishing the $N^2$
behaviour empirically would need either a larger sweep, since the traces' share is
still rising at the top of this one, or a measurement that isolates the trace
tensors from activations. We have neither, and we state the scaling claim as the
source paper's accounting rather than as our measurement. It follows that the
trace-factorization question this sweep was also meant to touch --- whether the
$\beta = 0$ path could be collapsed to $O(B \cdot N)$ --- cannot be settled by it
either, for the same reason: the traces are not the term being measured.

The practical reading is narrower but real: up to a thousand recurrent neurons
this pipeline fits comfortably in a consumer card's memory, and the constraint
that binds is time, not capacity --- with a cliff at the point where it stops
fitting, which is where the last row sits.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{When do local rules suffice?}
The results suggest the question is mis-posed. "Does rule X work" has three
qualifiers in this paper, and each of them moved the numbers more than the
difference between rules did.

\emph{Budget.} At 30 epochs every arm here is truncated; the gap between 30 and
250 epochs on the same seeds ($+7.95$ pp) is larger than any between-rule
difference we measured. A comparison run at a truncated budget therefore spends
most of its resolution measuring the truncation. This is not an argument against
short runs --- it is an argument for stating the budget in the claim.

\emph{Task.} The same e-prop configuration that reaches 0.8889 on sequential
MNIST does not learn SHD at all. That is not a rule property; it is what an
untuned configuration does when the task's time dimension and class count
change. Local rules are not task-portable out of the box, and a benchmark that
tests one task family should say so rather than generalise.

\emph{Composition.} W1's accuracy is a joint result of local features and a
supervised readout. Where a system has a non-local component, the number belongs
to the system --- and the interesting measurement is the ablation that separates
them, which is why we report both readouts rather than the better one.

\subsection{The interaction matters more than the rule}
Across the learning-rate grid, the rate itself moved the mean by $+2.38$ pp
while the neuron model moved it by 0.93--1.23 pp. Both are real; the first is
larger. The practical consequence is that a benchmark which fixes one
hyperparameter set for all rules measures its own choice at least as much as it
measures the rules --- and a reader cannot tell which from the reported number.

We therefore treat a hyperparameter sweep as part of the result rather than as
setup, and we report the sweep even where it complicates the story: the official
configuration here sits at the top of the grid, and we ran a pre-registered
probe specifically to check whether the grid edge was an artefact. A rule
reported at one setting is a point, not a claim.

\subsection{Limits}
Four boundaries, stated rather than implied.

\emph{Scale.} The accuracy results here are at 256 recurrent neurons. The claims
that motivate local rules --- on-chip learning, long sequences, energy --- are
about scales two to three orders of magnitude larger, and nothing in this paper
demonstrates that the behaviour transfers. We do have a resource scaling curve,
and it is informative about a ceiling rather than about the rules: up to 1{,}024
neurons the pipeline fits in a consumer card, and at 2{,}048 it spills out of one.
It also fails to corroborate the quadratic trace accounting, for a reason that
generalises --- at these sizes the activations, not the traces, are what fills
the card (Section~\ref{sec:results}). A reader looking for evidence that local
learning is cheap at scale will not find it here; a reader looking for where the
measurement stops will.

\emph{Substrate.} W2 is spiking; W1 and W3 are rate-based implementations of
rules whose source papers are defined on rate or on spikes respectively. The
"local" claim is about what the update rule touches, not about the neurons, and
readers looking for a spiking demonstration of W3 will not find one here.

\emph{Hardware and timing.} One consumer GPU, one machine. Wall-clock numbers
vary with what else the machine is doing --- we measured a factor of $1.5\times$
between a quiet machine and a loaded one on the same configuration --- so times
in this paper are reported as observations, not as benchmarks. Energy is not
reported at all (Section~\ref{sec:setup}: no power monitor, and a number
inferred from TDP would not be a measurement).

\emph{The comparison is against one control, not against the field.} The
same-budget BPTT arm is measured and reported (Section~\ref{sec:results}), and it
wins by 7.41 points. That is one control at one scale on one task family; it says
nothing about whether the gap persists at 50{,}000 neurons, on a task with
longer temporal structure, or against a differently tuned backprop baseline. It
does say that a paper reporting local-rule accuracy without a matched,
same-recipe backprop control is reporting a number with no denominator.

\section{Conclusion}
Honest measurement is a prerequisite for local-learning research, not a
housekeeping chore: at the budgets and seeds common in this literature, the
difference between two rules can be smaller than the difference the same rule
shows under two optimizers. This paper supplies one such measurement --- a
protocol, three rule lines, every number traceable to a versioned record, and a
negative result left standing --- on one consumer GPU. The obvious next step is
the one this design cannot answer: whether any of it holds at a scale where the
rules' own claims would matter, which would need a multi-layer network, a
scaling curve, and a task whose difficulty is not MNIST's.

\section{Triviality analysis details}
The two degeneracies in Section~\ref{sec:triviality} were found on synthetic
data, deliberately, before any of them could appear as a benchmark result.

\textbf{The toward-zero solution.} Two directly opposed update rules
($B = -A + \text{noise}$) were mixed with a scalar coefficient. Across five seeds
the conflict measure fell by 100\% --- every seed, making the reduction metric
look perfect --- while the coefficient itself drifted from 0.5 to 0.2--0.3,
i.e.\ the mixture was learning to stop using the second rule. The fix is not a
better metric but a joint one: the mixing objective carries an explicit minimum
retention term, and reductions are reported with the retained fraction beside
them. Without that pairing, "conflict reduced by 100\%" is a statement about the
solution's degeneracy rather than about the arbiter.

\textbf{The degenerate angle.} On a $(n_{\text{pre}}, n_{\text{post}})$ update
tensor, a per-synapse cosine is $\pm 1$ by construction, so any threshold on
"angle greater than $90^\circ$" reduces to a sign test. Taking the cosine across
a row (all outgoing weights of one presynaptic neuron) restores a real angle; a
column is a different question and gives a different answer, so the axis is
chosen explicitly and stated wherever a conflict number appears.

\textbf{A divergence that is not a result.} In the W1 ablation, the arm with
divisive normalization disabled diverged --- its local objective bottomed out at
epoch 10 and then climbed for the rest of training. The accuracy it produced is
retained in the records for traceability and is not reported as a measurement,
because a diverged run measures the divergence.
